Same moment, different attention

What each person notices in the photo. A dash means they don't notice it at all.

Nobody looks at the top of the shaft, which is the one place the camera was pointed.

Reflection

I chose The Landing because I was interested in how the reason you are in a space decides what you notice in it. I took the photograph in the Teacher Education Building during our class photography activity, thinking about Perec's line on p. 38, "We don't think enough about staircases." On the same page he says nothing is "uglier, colder, more hostile" than the staircases in today's apartment buildings, and this stairwell, all steel railings and beige walls, is that kind of staircase. Standing on the ground floor and pointing the camera straight up turned it into overlapping lines, window light, and one bright rectangle at the top. The webpage keeps that one photo the same while three invented people stand in the stairwell at 4:17 p.m.: someone coming home, someone visiting for the first time, and someone cleaning. Like the building Perec imagines with its front removed on p. 40, you can see all three at once.

Finding the angle took several tries. Looking straight up flattened the climb into planes, so the window, the steel stringers, and the pipes all fit in one frame, but it also made the route hard to follow. When I first showed the concept to my TA, Kalysta Fern, the drafts felt too uniform because all three voices described the same things. So I separated how each person thinks. The resident moves on muscle memory and errands and never looks at the window. The visitor is stuck on what to do next. The caretaker thinks in checklists, like Perec's inventories on pp. 41–43. I also gave each one a different sentence shape: long run-on sentences for the resident, questions for the visitor, and short lists for the caretaker. Leaving blanks for details a person skips made their attention feel believable. In revision I also had the three hear each other. The resident hears the visitor stopping on the stairs, the visitor hears the caretaker's bucket, the caretaker recognizes the resident's grocery bag, and all three hear the same door close at different volumes.

I'm happiest with the comparison, because reading across the table shows the pattern all at once. While revising I noticed that none of the three people ever looks at the top of the shaft, which is exactly where my camera was pointed, so I made that the one detail nobody notices. That connects to Perec's advice on p. 45 to "look upwards." I also cut a lot of extra features from earlier versions so that the photo and the stories are what you actually look at. If I kept working on it, I would record sound in the stairwell, like the door and footsteps, so you could hear how the same door sounds different from each floor.

Sources

Perec, Georges. Species of Spaces and Other Pieces. Edited with an introduction and translated by John Sturrock, Penguin, Penguin Twentieth-Century Classics, pp. 38, 40–45. Course PDF.

Outside the course reading: the building Perec plans on p. 40 became his novel Life A User's Manual, which is set at a single moment in one Paris apartment building (Godine's publisher description).
