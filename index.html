The Landing

Mehul Garg · ENG 169

Three people stand in this stairwell at the same moment, 4:17 p.m. Choose one to read what they notice.

The resident · Third-floor turn · 4:17 p.m.

The Turn Before My Own Door

The third floor requires a turn. The change of hand for the bag is guaranteed, alongside the twisty plastic that coils itself around your fingers. The left hand grips the rail while the right foot is already on the worn center of the next step. All the while you can look down at your groceries. Eggs, bread, milk. Is there any sweat running off your milk onto the receipt? Place your bag on your knee to feel.

Two floors below, the heavy door thuds. Don’t look back. You know someone has been working their way up and stopping, and now they’ve stopped. Grip the spot on the rail that everyone grips. Then the slight bump before the bracket. And the turn.

Black lines crossing over black lines above your head. Look down to stop them from changing. Your other hand feels odd. Empty. There should be another bag. The potatoes. But you left them at the checkout! Put the bag down on the landing and fumble for your keys. Six o’clock closing?

The visitor · One flight up from the entrance · 4:17 p.m.

Come Up When You Get Here

The text says, “Come up when you get here.” I paused one flight up. I frowned. How far up do I need to come? Wasn’t it 4C? That’s four floors up, isn’t it? My phone vibrated in my hand. Oh right, forgot to cancel this reminder. Thud thud thud on some metallic object above me. Bucket? I edged off to the side of the stairs and glanced down at my phone.

The entrance banged shut below me. 4:17. Why did I think I was supposed to be here at quarter past? I looked down again and could only see the turn of the stairs. There was no footfall. I looked straight up between the railings to see if anyone was leaning over. No one. Just railings.

Now I’m confused. Take the next flight. Stay to one side. Don’t shout people’s names! A narrow window, crossed with steel, ran up the right-hand side of the stairs. It was still afternoon outside. I typed “I’m here” and waited with my thumb hovering over the arrow.

The caretaker · Fourth-floor landing · 4:17 p.m.

The Rail After the Cloth Passes

Fourth-floor landing. Left balustrade is fine. There’s a thumb smudge on the inside bars. There is a tiny bit of grit on the floor by the wall. Fold the cloth again and work it into the joint. Flip the dirty side in. The handle of the bucket hits the railing. It’s too heavy to carry in one hand without spilling.

White piping can be seen above. Can’t reach the dust on the bend. Needs the long brush. One high spot on the right-hand glass. It’s behind a steel crossbar that isn’t easy to work around. Add to list on folded piece of paper. There’s some scuffing from below. A plastic bag rustles against the railing. 3B, back from the store.

There’s a small clunk going up the shaft. Hold the bucket still. Probably the door closer on the ground floor. No view of the entrance. There are footsteps lower down. Not one of ours. They stop every few stairs. Make sure the cloth is all tucked in. Wipe the wet edge. Next step is clear. Turn the handle of the bucket so it doesn’t snag any coats. Leave space for people to come around the corner.

Reflection

I chose The Landing because I was interested in how the reason you are in a space decides what you notice in it. I took the photograph in the Teacher Education Building during our class photography activity, thinking about Perec’s line on p. 38, “We don’t think enough about staircases.” On the same page he says that in today’s apartment buildings, nothing is “uglier, colder, more hostile” than the staircases, and this stairwell, all steel railings and beige walls, is that kind of staircase. Standing on the ground floor and pointing the camera straight up turned it into overlapping lines, window light, and one bright rectangle at the top. The webpage keeps that one photo the same while three invented people stand in the stairwell at 4:17 p.m.: someone coming home, someone visiting for the first time, and someone cleaning. As in the building Perec imagines with its front removed on p. 40, you can see all three at once.

Finding the angle took several tries. Looking straight up flattened the climb into planes, so the window, the steel stringers, and the pipes all fit in one frame, but it also made the route hard to follow. During the activity, I showed my TA, Kalysta Fern, the photo I had taken. As I continued working on the concept, I realized that the three voices needed to feel more distinct, so I focused on giving each person a different way of thinking and describing the scene. The resident moves on muscle memory and errands and never looks at the window. The visitor is stuck on what to do next. The caretaker thinks in checklists, like Perec’s inventories on pp. 41–43. I also gave each one a different sentence shape: second-person instructions for the resident, questions for the visitor, and short lists for the caretaker. Leaving blanks for details a person skips made their attention feel believable. In revision I also had the three hear each other. The resident hears the visitor stopping on the stairs, the visitor hears the caretaker’s bucket, the caretaker recognizes the resident’s grocery bag, and all three hear the same door close at different volumes.

I’m happiest with the comparison because reading across the table shows the pattern all at once. While revising, I noticed that none of the three people ever looks at the top of the shaft, which is exactly where my camera was pointed, so I made that the one detail nobody notices. That connects to Perec’s advice on p. 45 to “look upwards.” I also cut a lot of extra features from earlier versions so that the photo and the stories are what you actually look at. If I kept working on it, I would record sound in the stairwell, like the door and footsteps, so you could hear how the same door sounds different from each floor.
